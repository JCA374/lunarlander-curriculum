The network: what it is, how it thinks, how it evolved

The lander is flown by a small neural network: 15 numbers in, 4 scores out, every step, 50 times a second. This page takes it apart with live examples drawn from the real, current champion. Everything on the page is computed in your browser from the actual weights, so you can poke at it.

2. The 15 inputs, one by one

A neural network only knows what it's told. These are the numbers it gets every step. The first eight come straight from the game; the other seven are sensors computed from them, because the earlier wind project showed that what the network senses matters more than how big it is. The last column shows the values at one real moment of a flight (pick it in section 6).

How the drift sensor works, with numbers

Wind isn't one of the inputs. The lander has to notice it. Each step the sensor looks at how much the sideways speed changed, skipping steps where a side engine fired (that push would hide the wind):

change   = vx(now) − vx(before)             e.g. 0.0091 − 0.0000 = 0.0091
scaled   = change / (0.0008 × 15)           = 0.0091 / 0.012 = 0.76   (1.0 ≈ a full-strength gust)
drift    = drift + 0.15 × (scaled − drift)  = 0 + 0.15 × (0.76 − 0) = 0.114
next step, same push:                       0.114 + 0.15 × (0.76 − 0.114) = 0.211 … and so on toward 0.76

That last line is a running average: each step it moves 15% of the way toward the newest reading, so one noisy step can't fool it, and a steady push shows up within about 10 steps (a fifth of a second).

How the two route sensors work

waypoint_dx answers "how far am I from where I should be heading?". Normally that's the pad centre, so it equals x. But if a boulder is in the way and the lander is still above it, the waypoint moves to a point just beside the boulder, on the side the lander is already on. Once the lander is below the boulder, the waypoint snaps back to the pad.

above_boulder answers "how much height do I have to spare?". While the lander is horizontally over the boulder, it's the height above the boulder's top (plus a margin). Elsewhere it's 0. A negative value means danger: you are lower than the top of a rock you are over.

Both are computed from the other inputs with a few lines of fixed code. They don't add information; they re-express it, so that "go beside it, stay above it" becomes a straight-line rule a small network can learn, instead of a sharp if-then switch.

4. The squashing curve (and the bug it caused)

Hidden neurons pass their sum through tanh, an S-shaped curve that maps any number into the range −1 to +1. NEAT's version is tanh(2.5 × z): steeper than the textbook one. Without a squash like this, stacking neurons would add nothing: a sum of sums is still just a sum, a straight line. The bend is what lets a network make decisions like "if drifting and low, then…".

The flat ends are a trap.

The bug, 29 October. The copied pilot network gave the actions scores like main engine 14.5, left engine 9.5. NEAT's output neurons used tanh too, and both became exactly 1.0: a tie. The computer then picked the first of the tied actions (left engine), not the best one (main engine). About 1 step in 9 went wrong, so the copy that NEAT started from landed only 43% in wind instead of 92%. The fix: the output neurons now use no squash at all (the identity), so the scores pass through unchanged. Runs A, B and C started from the broken copy; run D from the exact one.

7. Where it came from: copying a pilot

NEAT usually starts from tiny networks with no hidden neurons and builds up. In the earlier wind project, it never built much: the best network stayed linear (no hidden neurons) and stalled at 91% landed. So this project gives evolution a head start.

Step 1, a pilot by hand. A short rule-based controller (in teacher.py). Its heart is a few lines like these:

aim       = beside the boulder if it's in the way, else the pad
lean      = 2.20 × (x − aim) + 2.01 × vx + 0.27 × drift      (capped at ±0.41)
height    = keep at least 1.07 × |x − aim| (and above the boulder top while over it)
if needing to climb more than to turn → main engine
elif leaning too far one way          → the opposite side engine

The numbers (2.20, 2.01, …) were tuned by another optimiser (CMA-ES) over 40 rounds of 16 candidates × 160 flights. Tuned, the pilot landed 100% in calm air, 93.8% in full wind, 74.2% with the boulder (500 unseen flights each).

Step 2, copy it into a network: behavioural cloning. Record the pilot flying (about 170,000 steps), and train a network with 24 hidden neurons to predict the pilot's action from the 15 inputs. That's ordinary supervised learning, like teaching a network to recognise digits: here the "label" is the pilot's choice.

Step 3, DAgger. A pure copy has a weakness: it has only seen the pilot's good flights. When the copy makes a small mistake it lands in a situation the pilot never got into, and it has no idea what to do. DAgger fixes that: let the copy fly, and at every step ask the pilot "what would you do here?". Add those answers to the training set and train again. Six rounds of this.

Step 4, hand it to NEAT. Every one of the 120 networks in NEAT's first generation is this copy, with tiny random changes (each weight nudged by about ±0.02). The 24 hidden neurons are protected: evolution may tune them and add new ones, but never delete them. (In the wind project's run K, evolution deleted every hidden neuron it was given within 400 generations.)

8. How NEAT changes a network

NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies, Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002) is evolution for neural networks. Each generation:

  1. every network flies the same 10 training flights (the same seeds, so the comparison is fair) and gets a fitness: the game's score + 100 for landing between the flags + up to 50 for landing near the centre − 100 for running out of time;
  2. networks are grouped into species of similar networks, so a new idea competes first with its own kind, not with the best of everything;
  3. the best of each species are copied unchanged (elitism), the top become parents, and the rest are replaced by children made by crossover and mutation.

Here is what each kind of change does, with an example. The probabilities are this run's actual settings.

Weight nudge (each weight, chance per child)

Add a small random number (spread ). Most of evolution's work is this: fine-tuning. Rarely () a weight is replaced with a fresh random value.

Add a neuron ( chance per child)

An existing connection is split in two, with a new neuron in the middle. The new path starts out doing nearly what the old one did, so the network isn't broken, but now there's a bend (the tanh) that evolution can exploit.

Add a connection ( chance per child)

Wire two unconnected neurons together, starting with a small random weight. In the start network every input already feeds every hidden neuron, so new connections mostly go input → output directly, or to and from new neurons.

Delete (neuron , connection )

The opposite moves. This keeps networks from growing without limit: extra parts only survive if they pay for themselves. Here the 24 original hidden neurons can't be deleted.

Crossover: how two parents make a child. Every connection gene has an identity (which neuron to which neuron). Genes the parents share are lined up and the child takes each one from a random parent; genes only one parent has come from the fitter parent.

Species: how different are two networks? NEAT's distance between two genomes: 
distance = c1 × (genes only one of them has) / (genes in the bigger genome)  +  c2 × (average weight difference on shared genes)
         =  × 6 / 470   (an example pair)  +   × 0.21  =  0.013 + 0.105  =  0.118

If the distance is below , they're the same species. Each species shares its fitness among its members, so one huge species can't take over the whole population, and a species that doesn't improve for generations is removed. Because every network here starts from the same copy, there are usually just one to three species.

Why so gentle? Stock NEAT nudges 80% of weights by about ±0.5 per child. That's fine for a tiny network still searching for any solution. 10. Check yourself

1. The network outputs four scores: −0.2, 3.1, 3.4, 1.0. What does the lander do?

It fires the main engine (the third score, 3.4, is the biggest). Only the order matters, not the size: 3.4 vs 3.1 is enough.

2. Why give the network a drift sensor instead of a bigger network?

Because the wind isn't in the observations at all; only its effect (a change in speed) is, and noticing a change needs memory of the step before. A feed-forward network has no memory. The sensor does the remembering for it. Earlier NEAT runs without it stalled near 85%.

3. A hidden neuron's inputs sum to 0.6 and its bias is −0.2. What does it output?

tanh(2.5 × (0.6 − 0.2)) = tanh(1.0) ≈ 0.76.

4. Why did the tanh on the outputs break the copied network?

Large scores (9.5 and 14.5) both squash to exactly 1.0, a tie, and the first tied action won instead of the best one.

5. What does DAgger add over plain copying?

It trains on the situations the copy gets itself into (with the pilot's answer for each), not just the ones the pilot visits. So the copy learns to recover from its own mistakes.

6. NEAT "adds a neuron" by splitting a connection with weight 0.84. What are the two new weights, and why?

1.0 into the new neuron and 0.84 out of it. The network then behaves almost the same as before (the tanh bends it a little), so the mutation doesn't wreck a working network, but evolution now has a new bend to work with.

7. Why are the 24 starting neurons protected from deletion?

In the wind project, evolution deleted every hidden neuron it was given within 400 generations, and ended up with a linear network stuck at 91%. Protecting them keeps the pilot's knowledge in place while evolution tunes it.
