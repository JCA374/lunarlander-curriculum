Lessons

The background, in five short pieces, while the training runs.

1. The task

Gymnasium's LunarLander: a lander starts at the top of the screen with a random push and must come to rest on the flat pad between two flags. Each step it can do one of four things: nothing, fire the left engine, the main engine, or the right engine. It sees eight numbers: position, velocity, angle, turn rate, and whether each leg touches the ground.

The game scores each flight: about +100 to +140 for flying to the pad and coming to rest, +100 for landing, −100 for a crash, and a small cost for fuel. A flight scoring 200 or more counts as "solved". This project counts something stricter: did it come to rest between the flags? The goal is 98 out of 100.

2. Wind, turbulence and the boulder

Wind (strength 15) is a sideways force that swells and fades in gusts. Turbulence (1.5) is a twisting force that tips the lander. Neither is visible in the eight numbers: the lander only notices the wind by drifting.

The boulder is new in this project: a rock, 1.6 to 2.6 m across, floating on the approach, up to 2.5 m to the side, its centre from 3 m above the pad up to where its top is still 3.5 m below the start. (Until 30 October it could sit right under the start, which made about 2% of flights close to unavoidable; that was fixed.) Often it sits right on the straight line down. Touching it is a crash. The lander is told where it is (three extra numbers: how far right, how far up, and how big).

3. Sensing the drift

The earlier wind project found that a lander which only sees its velocity stalls near 85%: by the time the velocity shows the wind, it's too late. A drift sensor fixes that. It watches how the sideways speed changes from step to step (ignoring steps when a side engine fired, since that push would hide the wind) and keeps a running average. It is an accelerometer, built only from the lander's own observations. A hand-written pilot went from 72% to 94% landed with it.

The network gets 13 numbers: the 8 observations, the drift sensor, the distance from the middle of the pad, and the boulder's position and size.

4. NEAT, started from a pilot

NEAT (NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies) evolves a population of neural networks: the best ones have children with small random changes (weights nudged, sometimes a new connection or a new neuron), and the population is split into species so new ideas get time to mature.

Plain NEAT starts from tiny networks with no hidden neurons, and in the wind project it kept them that way: a linear rule per action, stuck at 91%. Here NEAT starts from a good network with a hidden layer: 24 neurons trained to copy a hand-written pilot (behavioural cloning). The copying uses DAgger: the network flies, and at every state it reaches, the pilot says what it would have done, so the copy also learns to recover from its own mistakes. The seed neurons can't be deleted; evolution can tune them and add more.

5. The curriculum

Learning everything at once is hard. A curriculum builds up the difficulty: calm air, then a steady sideways push, then real gusts (wind 5 → 15), then turbulence on top (0.5 → 1.5), then the boulder, then a long polish with everything on. The population carries on from stage to stage.

Training wheels: in the wind stages, an invisible hand gently pulls the lander upright, fading from full to nothing over the stage. Checks are always flown without it.

Honest checks: a stage passes only when the champion lands often enough on 100 flights it has never seen, confirmed on a second fresh 100. The final goal is measured on 1,000 unseen flights, because champions chosen on few flights look better than they are (the winner's curse).
