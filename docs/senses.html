How the lander sees the world

You see a picture: hills, flags, a rock, a little craft fighting the wind. The lander sees none of that. Fifty times a second it gets 16 numbers, and that is its entire world. This page shows the two side by side, on a real flight of the current champion, so you can see what it knows, what it guesses, and what it is blind to.

2. The wind it can't see

The blue line is the sideways force the wind actually applies at each step. The lander never gets it. The red line is the drift sensor, and it is not a wind gauge. It's an accelerometer: it averages how fast the sideways speed changes, leaving out the steps when a side engine fired. So it feels the wind, but it also feels the lander's own main engine whenever the lander is tilted, because a tilted main engine pushes sideways too. In this flight the lander steers hard, and its own thrust (the big red swings) is larger than the wind itself.

That's still useful: "something is pushing me sideways, this hard" is exactly what it needs to know in order to lean back, and it's what lifted the hand-written pilot from 72% to 94% landings in wind. But a pure wind estimate would have to subtract the lander's own thrust, which this sensor doesn't do.

Update, 30 October: a better sensor. I measured each action's sideways push in calm air (the side engines ±0.0098 per step, the main engine −0.071 × sin(tilt)) and built a clean wind sensor that subtracts it, trusting main-engine steps ten times less because their random spread is as big as a full gust. Over 40 flights it correlates 0.97 with the true wind; the drift sensor only 0.14. It's the green line above (from run H on), and the 16th input. With it, the hand-written pilot went from 75% to 86% on the real task.

Turbulence is the same story, but twisting instead of pushing. The orange line below is the true turning force; the lander only feels it through its turn rate (green), after the fact.

4. Where the numbers come from, worked out

The game measures the world in metres: 20 m wide, 13.3 m tall, the pad at 3.33 m up, from x = 8 to 12. The network gets everything rescaled so that typical values sit between −1 and +1, which neural networks handle best. For the moment you've picked above:



Why rescale at all?

A neuron multiplies each input by a weight and adds them up. If one input ranged over thousands and another over hundredths, the big one
would swamp the small one until the weights learned to compensate, and learning would be slow and fragile. Keeping everything near ±1 puts all
inputs on an equal footing from the start. The game designers did this for the 8 standard numbers; the extra sensors here follow the same rule.


Why are the speeds such small numbers?

The game divides each speed by its 50 steps per second and multiplies by half the screen size. Sideways: vx = speed in m/s × 10 / 50,
so vx = 0.1 means 0.5 m/s. Vertical: vy = speed in m/s × 6.67 / 50, so vy = −0.3 means falling at about 2.25 m/s. Only consistency
matters to the network; it never needs to know the units.







5. Try it yourself


Watch the right-hand view with the left one covered (or squint). Could you land from that view alone? That's the lander's job, every
flight. Things to look for in the replay:


    

  • At the start, the red drift arrow is zero: it hasn't felt anything yet. Watch it grow when the lander tilts and fires its main engine: that's its own push, not the wind.
    • 

  • When the boulder is below it, the orange waypoint jumps to the side of the boulder. Once the lander is below the boulder, the waypoint snaps back to the pad.
    • 

  • When a leg touches, the legs' numbers flip from 0 to 1. That's its only sense of touch.
